# The UN Sustainable Development Goals

> **The bottleneck.** As designed: the absence of a shared global development agenda after the MDGs. What it actually supplies: a vocabulary, an indicator infrastructure, and a reporting ritual.

The best-documented natural experiment on whether **naming goals changes behaviour**. It is directly relevant because a manifesto is, structurally, a goal-setting document.

## Facts

**17 goals, 169 targets**, adopted September 2015 by all UN member states as the 2030 Agenda. **Indicators: 251 listed, of which 234 are unique** (13 repeat across targets), following refinements adopted at the 57th session of the UN Statistical Commission, March 2026. *(The commonly cited "~230" is stale.)*

**Enforcement: none.** A non-binding General Assembly resolution. No treaty obligation, no sanction, no compliance body, no dispute settlement. What replaces it: **Voluntary National Reviews** at the High-Level Political Forum — "voluntary, state-led… country-led and country-driven," i.e. states grade their own homework. **400+ VNR reports** submitted; 36 countries scheduled for HLPF 2026. There is no stated consequence for missing a target.

The actual enforcement substitutes are worth naming precisely, because they are real even if weak: reputational pressure at HLPF; statistical shaming through the annual progress report; **indicator infrastructure that makes non-performance legible**; and discursive uptake, so that NGOs, firms and ministries share a citable vocabulary.

**Progress, *SDG Report 2026* (released 7 July 2026)**, of the **139 targets with sufficient trend data**:

- **36%** on track or making moderate progress
- **49%** advancing too slowly
- **15%** regressed below their 2015 baselines

For trend: the 2025 report gave 35% / ~50% / 18%. Note also that only ~139 of 169 targets have trend data at all — **roughly 18% of the agenda cannot be graded.**

## Scope: how wide, and what holds it there

**Nothing.** 169 unweighted targets with no priority ordering and known internal trade-offs — growth against emissions, land use against biodiversity. The consequence is subtle and worth stating: **no actor can be held to have failed at "the SDGs," because the portfolio is large enough that every actor can find a target it is advancing.** Unbounded scope is not merely inefficient; it is an accountability solvent.

## Verdict, and the structural reason

**An extraordinarily successful coordination and vocabulary device; a failed delivery mechanism.** After eleven of fifteen years, about a third of gradeable targets are on track.

**Structural reason:** the SDGs decoupled goal-setting from the three things that make goals bind — (1) a compliance mechanism, (2) attached financing, (3) an allocation of responsibility to a specific actor. What remains is self-reported, self-scheduled review, which rewards reporting effort rather than performance.

**The definitive empirical test.** Biermann, Hickmann, Sénit et al., "Scientific evidence on the political impact of the Sustainable Development Goals," *Nature Sustainability* (2022) — a systematic review of **over 3,000 peer-reviewed studies, 2016 to April 2021**. Finding: the SDGs have had *"only limited transformative political impact… thus far"*, and the impact has been **"largely discursive, affecting the way actors understand and communicate about sustainable development"** rather than producing normative or institutional change "from legislative action to changing resource allocation." This largely fails the "governance through goals" hypothesis that Kanie and Biermann advanced in 2017.

## Against me

- **A manifesto is a goal-setting document, and this is the largest goal-setting experiment ever run.** Its measured effect is discursive. That is the honest prior for what a manifesto can achieve, and it should be stated in the piece rather than left for a reviewer.
- **Breadth destroys accountability, mechanically.** Ten departments across medicine, energy, environment and AI is a small SDG list. Ask directly: what would count as this institution having failed? If no answer, the breadth has already done its damage.
- **"Who should do what" is exactly what the SDGs omit** — and it is exactly what *Issues in Science and Technology* asks a proposal to specify. That is not a coincidence; it is the discipline that separates the two genres.

## Implication

**Q1 and Q2.** Two rules follow. First, the manifesto must attach **responsibility to a named actor**, not to humanity. Second, it must state a **failure condition** — what observation in 2035 would mean this was wrong. Both are cheap to write and both are what the SDGs lack.
