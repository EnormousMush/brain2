# The Usefulness of Useless Knowledge — Abraham Flexner (1939)

> **What this is for me.** A masterclass in rhetorical construction, and a cautionary case in how an argument can immunise an institution against ever being evaluated.

## Position the author wrote from

Founding Director of the Institute for Advanced Study, 1930–39. **This is a fundraising and legitimation document written by a sitting institution head**, published nine years after the institution opened. Read it as such: the argument was reverse-engineered from an existing design.

## How it opens

Not with a claim — with a **paradox posed against the backdrop of 1939**:

> "IS it not a curious fact that in a world steeped in irrational hatreds which threaten civilization itself, men and women — old and young — detach themselves wholly or partly from the angry current of daily life to devote themselves to the cultivation of beauty, to the extension of knowledge, to the cure of disease, to the amelioration of suffering, just as though fanatics were not simultaneously engaged in spreading pain, ugliness, and suffering?"

The reader is invited to resolve a puzzle. Flexner then supplies the resolution. Note how much work this does compared with asserting that basic research is valuable.

## The argument, in four steps

1. **Pose the paradox.** Amid civilisational crisis, people withdraw into apparently useless inquiry. Is this frivolous?
2. **Argue by historical example that the "useless" was in every case the source of the "useful"** — and that the useful was invisible to the person doing the useless work. The demonstrator never sought the application; the applier added comparatively little.
3. **Generalise the mechanism.** Utility is unforecastable, so *selecting for utility ex ante systematically destroys the pipeline*. Freedom of inquiry is therefore not a perk but a production condition. Corollary: the true enemy of civilisation is organised suppression of thought — with 1939 Germany as the implicit demonstration.
4. **Instantiate** — present the IAS as the institutional form the argument implies, and then **explicitly refuse to justify it on the utility grounds just established.** This is the essay's most sophisticated move and its most consequential one.

**The closing, verbatim:**

> "We make ourselves no promises, but we cherish the hope that the unobstructed pursuit of useless knowledge will prove to have consequences in the future as in the past. Not for a moment, however, do we defend the Institute on that ground. It exists as a paradise for scholars who, like poets and musicians, have won the right to do as they please and who accomplish most when enabled to do so."

## The evidentiary base — the whole of it

| example | what it demonstrates |
|---|---|
| **Maxwell → Hertz → Marconi** | the theorist and the experimentalist did the work; the commercial inventor added an increment. The set-piece of the essay. |
| **Faraday** | pure curiosity about electricity and magnetism → the electrical industry |
| **Gauss, non-Euclidean geometry** | pure abstraction → the mathematics of relativity |
| **Group theory** | abstract algebra → quantum spectroscopy |
| **Calculus of probability** | originated in gambling → the foundation of insurance |
| **Einstein, 1924–25 gas theory** | then-useless → later explains liquid helium |
| **Paul Ehrlich** | staining techniques → bacteriology and chemotherapy |
| **Count Chardonnet** | accidental discovery → rayon |

**Rhetorical strategy in one line: proof by enumeration of survivorship.** A list of cases where the useless became useful, with no accounting of the far larger set where it did not. That is simultaneously the essay's persuasive engine and its logical weak point.

## What actually happened

The IAS, already nine years old, got its founding myth. The essay became the most-cited defence of curiosity-driven research in the English language and is still handed out by research institutions today. It did not itself found anything.

## What a critic says

- **Richard Feynman**, "The Dignified Professor," in *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* — the canonical rebuttal, aimed at the institution rather than the essay: freedom from all obligation removes the friction that generates problems worth solving. See `institute-for-advanced-study.md` for the quotation.
- **The survivorship problem.** Eight successes and none of the vastly larger population of inquiries that stayed useless. As stated, it is an unfalsifiable defence of any research whatsoever.
- **The immunity problem, which is the important one.** Flexner's closing refusal — "not for a moment do we defend the Institute on that ground" — is elegant and institutionally convenient: it exempts the institution from evaluation on any ground at all. **Every subsequent claim that a research institute cannot be measured traces to that paragraph.**

## What to steal

- **Open with a puzzle the reader wants resolved**, not a thesis they must accept.
- **Argue by cases, but be honest about the denominator** — the fix for the survivorship problem is one sentence acknowledging it, and it costs almost nothing.
- **Refuse an argument you could have used.** The closing move is powerful precisely because it gives something up. Use the technique; do not use it to escape evaluation.
- **Note the sequencing of the institution itself, not the essay:** IAS opened with mathematics — the cheapest school, no laboratories, at the moment the global talent pool was most dislocated. Start where capital cost is lowest and talent is most available.

## Against me

- **This is the argument the manifesto will be accused of making**, and the accusation comes with Feynman attached. Meet it explicitly.
- **An argument that exempts an institution from evaluation is a liability, not an asset.** Combined with `un-sdgs.md` and `gates-grand-challenges.md`, the pattern is consistent: institutions that cannot fail do not improve. Specify the failure condition.
- **The essay justifies freedom, not breadth.** It is not evidence for a ten-department institute. It is evidence for leaving individual scholars alone — a different proposal.
