# Asilomar 1975

> **The bottleneck.** A field had to find an exit condition from a moratorium it had imposed on itself, before an outside authority imposed one instead.

The most-cited precedent in AI governance and, as of its fiftieth anniversary in 2025, the most thoroughly indicted. **Anyone writing about how humanity collectively handles a technology in 2026 is writing into this argument whether they know it or not.**

## Facts

**The 1974 Berg letter.** "Potential Biohazards of Recombinant DNA Molecules," *Science*, 26 July 1974, p. 303, also in *Nature* and *PNAS*. Eleven signatories chaired by **Paul Berg**, including Baltimore, Boyer, Cohen, Nathans and Watson. Four recommendations: voluntary deferral of two experiment types; caution on a third; **an NIH advisory committee**; and **an international scientific meeting the following year**. That fourth clause is Asilomar.

**Was the moratorium observed?** *"Universally observed, in spite of disagreements about its concerns and methods."* This is the single most consequential empirical fact about Asilomar and the one most often asserted without qualification. **The qualification:** European scientists, notably Ken Murray in Edinburgh, saw an American "plot" to control the field, and noted in *Nature* that signatories **Cohen and Boyer had already done experiments the moratorium was meant to prevent.** Some signatories were added after the drafting meeting they never attended.

**The conference: 24–27 February 1975**, Asilomar Conference Grounds, Pacific Grove. Called by Berg and **Maxine Singer** with Baltimore and others.

**Composition — the number that carries the whole argument:**

- ~**90 American scientists** plus ~**60 from 12 other countries** (~150 total)
- **4 lawyers** — including Daniel Singer, Maxine Singer's husband
- **16 journalists**, embargoed from reporting until after the conference
- **Zero** public-interest, patient, labour, environmental or Global South representatives

Four lawyers, advisory on liability, not representative. Sixteen journalists — four times as many — admitted precisely so the *outcome* would be reported, and embargoed so the *process* would not be. **The ratio encodes the governance model: the press was present in quantity to legitimate the decision; non-scientists in token numbers to inform it. The public was an audience, not a party.**

**What it decided.** Research continues under stringent restrictions, with risk-tiered containment on two axes — four levels of physical containment and three of biological. Certain experiments prohibited outright. Note a substantive technical failure rarely mentioned in the celebratory retellings: **biological containment — organisms deliberately crippled so they could not survive outside the lab — proved less effective than believed.**

**The vote.** The organisers *intended to avoid one*, preferring to issue recommendations as their own statement. Participants insisted; Berg took a show of hands on 27 February and the recommendations passed overwhelmingly. **The statement was then edited after the vote**, causing dissatisfaction — critics noted the organisers controlled the output despite the appearance of consensus.

**Where bindingness actually entered.** The **NIH Guidelines of 23 June 1976** made the containment protocols **mandatory for grant recipients**. That, not Asilomar, is the enforcement mechanism. **Anyone not taking NIH money was never bound at all** — the loophole through which the commercial biotech industry subsequently walked.

## The fiftieth-anniversary indictment

By 2025 this is a coordinated scholarly programme, not scattered dissent. Central venue: **"Legacies of Asilomar," special issue, *Journal of the History of Biology* 58(1), 2025**, introduced by Luis Campos and Francesco Cassata. Their thesis: Asilomar has become a **ritualised touchstone that obscures its actual complexity**, and invocations of it for CRISPR and AI "rely on simplified, distorted interpretations disconnected from the conference's original concerns and context."

**J. Benjamin Hurlbut** (ASU), *Science*, 30 January 2025 — "Taking responsibility: Asilomar and its legacy." The myth is a meeting where scientists took responsibility and secured public trust. The reality is that the self-governance model *"has engendered reactive distrust"*; it *"may elicit public acquiescence and secure scientific autonomy in the short run"* while failing democratic accountability. **The sharp version: Asilomar did not build trust. It bought autonomy, and the bill came due later.**

**Shobita Parthasarathy** (Michigan), *Science* editorial, 31 January 2025 — "Viewing Asilomar from the Global South." *"Only scientists attended Asilomar,"* and the composition determined the agenda. It reinforced the premise that *"only those with relevant scientific expertise should guide regulations."* It omitted agricultural consolidation, privatisation of the public domain, and ethics. *"Western scientists dismissed the Global South's historical experiences and understanding of the issues"* — producing later regulatory divergence the West had not anticipated. The lasting damage: it *"reinforced an approach to controversial technologies that centers the knowledge of technical experts instead of publics,"* and publics *"feel like they are fundamentally affected by science and technology policies, but they have little opportunity to shape them."*

Her AAAS 2025 session title is the argument in six words: **"Who Is In the Room When We Talk About AI?"**

**Jasanoff, Hurlbut & Saha**, "CRISPR Democracy," *Issues in Science and Technology* — the foundational statement of the misapplication thesis. *"Questions about biosecurity and ethics were explicitly excluded from the agenda."* *"Limiting risk to accidental releases of pathogens left untouched the economic, social, and political implications."* The core sentence: **"Asilomar shows how under the guise of responsible self-regulation science steps in to shape the forms of governance."** And the warning: *"An uncritical application of the Asilomar model to CRISPR would do a disservice to history as well as democracy."*

**Nature editorial, 8 April 2025** — credits "courage and foresight," faults the absence of low- and middle-income countries, and notes an undisclosed commercial dimension: universities gained the ability to patent discoveries and hold IP, which was not transparently acknowledged at the time.

## Why the analogy to AI fails structurally

1. **Asilomar's participants were nearly all federally funded academics, and the enforcement mechanism was a grant condition.** AI's frontier developers are commercial and not grant-dependent. **The coupling that made Asilomar binding does not exist.**
2. **Berg himself says so.** *"It would be much more difficult to organize such an event today"* because of corporate involvement and economic interests.
3. **Asilomar's hazard was bounded and physical** — a pathogen escaping a room. Containment is a meaningful concept for a bacterium. The critics' charge is that even then the risk framing was too narrow; applied to a general-purpose technology whose harms are economic, political and distributional, the framing does not merely narrow — it misses.

Note that the **Asilomar AI Principles (2017)** were produced at the same physical venue, an explicit borrowing of the brand. See `_ai-era-landscape.md`.

## Verdict, and the structural reason

**Mixed. It solved the problem it defined and the definition was the problem.** The conference converted a diffuse anxiety into a tractable technical question, answered it competently, and handed the answer to a funder who made it enforceable. That is a real achievement and it is why the story is told. The fifty-year critique is not that the answer was wrong but that **the act of scoping — done by the people with the most to lose from a wider scope — was itself the governance decision**, and nobody outside the room voted on it.

## Against me

This is the most direct challenge to a "how humanity progresses together" manifesto, and it comes in two directions at once.

- **If the document proposes that the technically expert should convene and decide, Parthasarathy and Hurlbut have already written the review.** "Who is in the room" is now the first question anyone asks of this genre, and the honest answer for most manifestos is "the author, and people like the author."
- **But the counter-lesson is real too:** the room *did* deliver. A hundred and fifty scientists in four days produced a workable containment regime that a funding agency could enforce a year later. Inclusive processes with hundreds of stakeholders have not reliably produced comparable artifacts. **A serious manifesto has to hold both facts, not pick one.**
- **And the most uncomfortable point:** what made Asilomar binding was neither the argument nor the assembly. It was money with conditions attached. If the manifesto wants an effect, it should ask where its equivalent grant condition is — and if the answer is nowhere, say so.

## Implication

**Q1 and the objections file.** Two tests any draft must pass: *who is in the room, and who decided the scope?* Add both to `00-argument/objections.md`. And note the sequencing lesson — Asilomar happened because a moratorium needed an exit condition. **A self-imposed constraint created the demand for the meeting.** No constraint, no meeting.
