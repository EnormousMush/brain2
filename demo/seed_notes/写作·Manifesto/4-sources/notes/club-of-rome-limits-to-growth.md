# The Limits to Growth (1972)

> **What this is for me.** The control case. It is the civilizational-scale document that made **falsifiable** claims — and the fifty-year record of what happened to it is the best available evidence on whether that is a good idea.

## What it claimed, and what was attributed to it

Commissioned by the **Club of Rome** (founded 1968 by Aurelio Peccei and Alexander King), modelled with **World3** at MIT. It presented **numbered scenarios**, not predictions. The authors said so at the time and have said so ever since. What was attributed to it — and what it was refuted for — was a set of dated predictions of resource exhaustion it did not make.

## The reception, in three acts

**Act 1 — attacked on the merits, immediately, by name.** Within twelve months: Peter Passell in the *New York Times*, **William Nordhaus**, and the Sussex SPRU unit. The methodological objections were substantially correct on their own terms: World3 had **no prices, no markets, no endogenous substitution**, and unestimated parameters. **Nordhaus's objection has never been rebutted — it has been sidestepped**, by arguing that aggregate behaviour-mode matters more than parameter fidelity.

**Act 2 — the caricature wins.** **Julian Simon**, *The Ultimate Resource* (1981): "resource" is a time-varying category, human ingenuity creates resources, and price drives substitution. The **Simon–Ehrlich wager** (formalised 29 September 1980, settled 1990, $1,000 across copper, chromium, nickel, tin, tungsten) was won by Simon; Ehrlich paid $576.07.

**The caveat almost never cited:** Kiel et al. (2010) found that across all possible ten-year windows from 1900 to 2008, **Simon would have won only 38.4% of the time.** Extending to 1980–2011 reverses the result on four of five metals. **The wager is evidence about 1980–1990, not about resource economics** — and it settled the public argument for thirty years anyway.

**Act 3 — the late audits.** **Graham Turner** (CSIRO, 2008; Melbourne Sustainable Society Institute, 2014) compared the scenarios against 1970–2010 data and found the business-as-usual run "aligns well with historical data," with "the general onset of collapse first appear[ing] at about 2015." **Gaya Herrington** (2020–21) repeated the exercise with more recent data. Both authors are careful: the model outputs are *"indications of the system's behavioral tendencies only"*; the scenarios *"were not meant to produce point predictions"*; the steepness of any decline *"cannot be used for predictive purposes."*

**And the model was most wrong in an instructive way:** the binding constraint turned out to be **sinks** (pollution, climate) rather than **sources** (minerals) — which Turner himself flagged.

## The lesson: what happens to a document that makes falsifiable claims

1. **It gets attacked on the merits, immediately and by name.** That is a **feature**: it means the document entered the domain of argument rather than the domain of sentiment. Nobody attacks a document that cannot be wrong.
2. **It gets refuted for a claim it did not make.** The dated-resource-exhaustion caricature is what most educated people believe *Limits* said.
3. **The refutation sticks harder than the original.** Simon–Ehrlich is remembered; Kiel et al. is not.
4. **Audits can rehabilitate it — partially, and far too late.** Turner and Herrington restored its standing among sustainability scholars three to five decades on, long after the policy window closed. Richard Heinberg: *"Policy makers largely ignored the work thereafter."*

## What to steal

- **Making a falsifiable claim is correct and it is expensive.** It buys you a real argument and it costs you a caricature you will never fully escape. Decide deliberately, not by default.
- **If you make one, state the failure condition in the document itself, in the author's own words, prominently.** *Limits* did this and it did not help — because the failure condition was buried in a book and the caricature fit in a sentence. **Put yours in a sentence.**
- **Expect the sink, not the source.** The variable you have the best data on is usually not the binding one.

## Against me

- **This is the strongest argument for making claims that can be checked, and the strongest evidence about what it costs.** A manifesto that says nothing checkable will never be refuted and will also never be argued with — see `bush-1945-as-we-may-think.md`, which is the opposite pole.
- **Being right late is close to being wrong.** Fifty years of vindication produced no policy change. If the point is influence rather than vindication, the timing of the claim matters more than its truth.
- **The Club of Rome was, by design, a "non-organisation."** It produced an enormous discourse and no institution. Directly relevant to whether the manifesto's institution is the ask or the illustration.
