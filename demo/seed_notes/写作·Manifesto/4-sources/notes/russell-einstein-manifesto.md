# The Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955)

> **What this is for me.** The closest structural model available. A short philosophical document, written by people with no power, addressed to humanity as such, in the face of a technology its own authors had helped create — which produced a working institution within two years and a Nobel Peace Prize within forty.

If the manifesto has one model, it is this and not Bush. Read it before drafting a line.

## Position the authors wrote from

Eleven private individuals, nine of them Nobel laureates at signing. **They held no office and represented nobody** — and the document says so in its second paragraph, deliberately. Joseph Rotblat, who chaired the launch press conference and later built the institution, was the only scientist who left the Manhattan Project on moral grounds and the only signatory besides Leopold Infeld without a Nobel. His came in 1995, for the institution this document created.

**The timeline matters.** Russell wrote to Einstein on 11 February 1955; Einstein replied endorsing it on 16 February; Russell sent the draft on 5 April; **Einstein signed on 11 April and died on 18 April.** Russell learned of the death mid-flight from Rome to Paris, assumed the plan had collapsed, and found Einstein's letter of approval waiting at his hotel. Einstein's words on signing: *"I am gladly willing to sign your excellent statement."*

Around eighteen scientists were approached. **Niels Bohr declined**, fearing such statements might undermine UN efforts. **Otto Hahn refused**, preferring the separate Mainau Declaration.

## How it opens

The first paragraph is one sentence, and **the opening move is the ask**:

> "In the tragic situation which confronts humanity, we feel that scientists should assemble in conference to appraise the perils that have arisen as a result of the development of weapons of mass destruction, and to discuss a resolution in the spirit of the appended draft."

It does not open with the threat. It opens with a proposed meeting. Then, immediately:

> "We are speaking on this occasion, not as members of this or that nation, continent or creed, but as human beings, members of the species Man, whose continued existence is in doubt."

## The argument, in twelve steps

1. **Standing.** Scientists must convene to appraise the perils. *(The ask precedes the argument.)*
2. **Suspension of nationality.** The speakers disclaim national, continental and creedal identity.
3. **Cognitive demand.** *"We have to learn to think in a new way"* — specifically to stop asking *"what steps can be taken to give military victory to whatever group we prefer, for there no longer are such steps."*
4. **Diagnosis of public error.** The public imagines nuclear war as city destruction scaled up.
5. **The physical mechanism that defeats intuition.** Fallout. Bikini showed bombs send *"radio-active particles into the upper air"* producing *"deadly dust or rain."* Harm is delivered by dispersal, not blast — so it is not geographically bounded.
6. **Magnitude.** *"It is stated on very good authority that a bomb can now be manufactured which will be 2,500 times as powerful as that which destroyed Hiroshima."*
7. **Escalation to the species.** A nuclear war *"might possibly put an end to the human race."*
8. **The disjunction.** *"Shall we put an end to the human race: or shall mankind renounce war?"*
9. **Foreclosure of the easy answer — the load-bearing step.** Peacetime bans are not a solution: *"Whatever agreements… had been reached in time of peace, they would no longer be considered binding in time of war."* This forecloses arms control as a terminal answer and forces the argument all the way to war itself.
10. **The residual value of partial measures.** Nuclear agreements still reduce tension and fear of surprise attack. *(A deliberate concession that reopens a practical path.)*
11. **Neutrality on ideology.** Disputes must be settled peacefully regardless of which political system one prefers.
12. **The appeal.**

> "There lies before us, if we choose, continual progress in happiness, knowledge, and wisdom."
> "Shall we, instead, choose death, because we cannot forget our quarrels?"
> "We appeal, as human beings, to human beings: Remember your humanity, and forget the rest."
> "If you can do so, the way lies open to a new Paradise; if you cannot, there lies before you the risk of universal death."

## The move on national loyalty — three manoeuvres, not one

This is the most transferable technique in the document and it is worth taking apart.

- **Renunciation of representative status.** *"not as members of this or that nation, continent or creed."* The signatories strip themselves of the standing that would make them negotiators. **This is what licensed a French communist (Joliot-Curie) and an American cold warrior (Bridgman) to co-sign.**
- **Substitution of a biological category for a political one.** *"members of the species Man."* The replacement constituency is a taxon, not a polity. The shared predicate is survival, not interest.
- **Symmetry of address.** *"We appeal, as human beings, to human beings."* Speaker and audience become the same category. **The document's authority is technical in the middle (steps 5–7) and explicitly non-technical at the end.** It claims no privileged position where it matters most.

Russell's reason was operational, not stylistic: he needed signatories from both blocs, and any nationally inflected phrasing would have cost him one side.

## The concrete ask

**A named action, no named institution.**

- **The ask is a meeting.** No body, venue, convenor, date, membership or budget.
- **What is asked of governments** — in the appended Resolution — is *"to realize, and to acknowledge publicly"* that their purpose cannot be furthered by world war. The only verb directed at states is **acknowledge**. An epistemic and rhetorical act, not a legal one.
- **To the reader:** a question.

## What it deliberately did not do

At ~2,280 words it omits: any verification scheme; all numbers except one; any named adversary — neither the USSR nor the USA is identified as a threat; any proposed institution; all legal language; any implementation path; and any endorsed policy alternative.

**It is a warrant, not a plan.** It establishes standing and a problem and delegates the solution to a meeting that did not yet exist.

## Binding on nobody, effective anyway — the mechanism

Four channels:

1. **Borrowed authority, not delegated authority.** The signatories had no power to bind, so instead they made non-agreement into a position that had to be defended.
2. **Cross-bloc composition as proof-of-concept.** A Soviet-aligned communist co-signing with American, British, German, Polish and Japanese scientists *was itself the demonstration* that a transnational scientific constituency existed. **The signature list is an argument, not a formality.**
3. **A last-signature effect.** Einstein's death seven days after signing turned the document into a testament and made the press conference newsworthy independent of its content.
4. **Deliberate under-specification — the decisive one.** Because it asked only for *a conference*, the ask was cheap enough for a private individual to satisfy. **Cyrus Eaton offered to finance and host it on 13 July 1955, four days after the launch.** A demand for a treaty could have been met by nobody. A demand for a meeting could be met by one rich man with a house.

## What to steal

- **Open with the ask, not the threat.** One sentence, and it is a request for a specific human action.
- **Renounce representative standing explicitly.** It is what makes a cross-bloc coalition signable, and it converts weakness (no office, no mandate) into the document's central rhetorical asset. **Directly applicable to a young author with no institutional position.**
- **Be technical in the middle and human at the end.** Claim expert authority only for the mechanism (steps 5–7), and none at all for the appeal.
- **Foreclose the easy answer.** Step 9 is what stops the document being answerable with "we'll sign a treaty." Every manifesto needs the equivalent — the obvious solution, named and shown to be insufficient.
- **Size the ask so one person can satisfy it.** This is the single most underrated design choice here. Under-specification was not vagueness; it was making the demand cheap enough to be met.

## Against me

- **The technology supplied the argument's spine, and AI does not supply the same one.** Steps 5–7 work because fallout is a *physical* mechanism that defeats intuition and can be stated in one paragraph with one number. There is no equally clean physical mechanism for AI. The equivalent has to be constructed, and constructing it is most of the work.
- **The species-survival framing was true and unusually available.** "Shall we put an end to the human race" was a literal question in 1955. Reaching for it without that literalness reads as inflation — and in 2026 it is already occupied by the Center for AI Safety's twenty-two-word statement and by Yudkowsky and Soares.
- **Nine Nobel laureates.** The mechanism was *borrowed authority*, and a manifesto without signatories has to borrow it from somewhere else — which in practice means from the argument, from first-hand material, or from nowhere.
- **The ask was a meeting because the authors could not do more.** Note what happened next: the meeting produced an institution whose most documented successes were *technical* (the seismic black-box formula) rather than moral. See `pugwash.md`, which is where the moral appeal actually cashed out — and where its limits show.
