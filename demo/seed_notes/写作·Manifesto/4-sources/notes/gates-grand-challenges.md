# Gates Grand Challenges in Global Health

> **The bottleneck.** As designed: fourteen "difficult and discrete roadblocks to progress" in global health that no single funder was attacking. As it turned out: the roadblocks were not where the design said they were.

The most important cautionary case for any document that proposes to name humanity's hardest problems and fund work on them.

## Facts

Announced by Bill Gates at Davos, **January 2003**; fourteen challenges published in *Science*, October 2003. **$200M** from BMGF to the Foundation for the NIH, raised to **$450M** in May 2005; co-funders Wellcome Trust ($27.1M) and CIHR ($4.5M). **43 projects, $436.6M awarded June 2005**, scientists from 33 countries. Selection funnel: 1,000+ challenge ideas → **1,500+ letters of intent from 75 countries** → 405+ full proposals → seven peer panels, 150+ experts. Governance via a Scientific Board chaired by Harold Varmus, administered through FNIH; grants went mostly to elite Western institutions.

At twenty years (October 2023): **$1.6B invested, 3,800 grants, 118 countries** across the whole Grand Challenges family. Grand Challenges Explorations, launched 2008, moved to small fast open $100k grants — a structural admission that the few-large-bets design was not working.

## Scope: how wide, and what holds it there

Framed explicitly as **technical** roadblocks. That framing *was* the scope enforcement, and it is exactly what went wrong: it drew the boundary around discovery and put everything downstream outside the institution.

An **Ethical, Social and Cultural programme was added in 2005** — an internal acknowledgment that delivery and acceptability barriers had been omitted from the original design.

## Verdict, and the structural reason

**Scientifically productive, translationally disappointing on its own original terms.** No product from the 43 original projects has been shown to have reached routine use in the intended settings, and the foundation reoriented toward delivery and toward many-small-bets.

**The structural reason, stated plainly: it defined the problem as a discovery bottleneck and built a discovery instrument.** The binding constraint was downstream — manufacturing, cold chain, regulatory approval, procurement, absent markets, health-system capacity. A five-year research grant to a university PI has no mechanism to carry a candidate across a ten-to-twenty-year translational valley. Compounding it, no independent evaluation was designed in at the outset.

**The admissions, which are worth quoting because they come from inside:**

- **Bill Gates**, at the tenth anniversary, October 2014: *"I have to say at the time I was pretty naive about how long that process would take"* — from invention to lives saved. He acknowledged underestimating the effort of implementing new technologies in countries lacking basic services. He reportedly graded the early programme **a "C"**, faulting concentration of large grants in too few hands and the flow of funding to Western rather than developing-world scientists.
- **Steven Buchsbaum**, BMGF Deputy Director: on thermostable vaccines, *"Although individual projects made a lot of progress, for a large part they are not on a path to changing the vaccine supply chain as of today."* He admitted the foundation *"had not been precise enough in asking how thermostable does a vaccine have to be"* to change market and delivery conditions — **a specification failure, not a science failure.** And: the shift from a technology focus to a delivery focus *"has not occurred more broadly within the foundation."*
- **David McCoy** (Queen Mary University of London): *"There has been very little in the way of any independent evaluation of the Grand Challenges programme."*
- **Anne-Emanuelle Birn**, *The Lancet* (2005), "Gates's grandest challenge: transcending technology as public health ideology": the initiative substitutes "the power of science" for economic, social and political causation. Malnutrition is political and economic, not a technical roadblock.

## Against me

- **This is the failure mode a "hardest problems" manifesto is most likely to reproduce.** Naming the problems is the easy part; the hard part is owning the span between an invention and its use, and a research institute is structurally the wrong instrument for the second half.
- **"How much of X do we need to change the system?" is the question that was skipped.** Buchsbaum's thermostability admission is the sharpest lesson in this note: a technical target specified without a system-change threshold produces progress that changes nothing.
- **Build the independent evaluation in at the start, or it will never happen.** Twenty years and $1.6B later, McCoy's complaint still stands. A manifesto that proposes a permanent institution should specify who audits it and on what schedule, in the founding document, because nobody adds that later.

## Implication

**Q1.** The diagnosis must state **which end of the gap the institution owns** — discovery or deployment — and if it claims both, it must name the instrument that spans them. This is the same defect as the SDGs, from the opposite direction: Grand Challenges funded an input and specified an outcome; the SDGs specified an outcome and funded nothing.
