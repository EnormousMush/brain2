# Speculative Technologies

> **The bottleneck.** Run coordinated research programmes *across multiple existing organisations* — the DARPA programme-manager function, decoupled from any single lab and applied to materials and manufacturing, where no one institution owns the whole stack.

The third scope model in the vault, and the one closest to a workable form of "many problems, one roof."

## Facts

Launched **15 February 2023** by **Ben Reinhardt** as a 501(c)(3). Backers include Patrick Collison, Malcolm Handley, Protocol Labs, Schmidt Futures, the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation and Open Philanthropy; amounts not disclosed. Programme economics: **~$10M per programme over ~5 years, with an explicitly assumed ~10% success rate.**

**Active programmes:** Carbon to Chemicals (cell-free bioreactors, sugar and CO₂ to chemicals, replacing petroleum feedstocks); Impossible Fibers (biological mechanisms for high-performance fibers); Nanomodular Electronics (customisable microelectronics without silicon etching); Molecular Additive Manufacturing (nanoscale-precision heterogeneous materials, toward atomically precise manufacturing). Plus the **Brains Coordinated Research Accelerator** — a 15-week part-time programme, self-described as "YCombinator for coordinated research programs," which is the intake funnel for new programme leads. Also an ARIA collaboration building a public resource library on coordinated research programme management.

## Scope: how wide, and what holds it there

**Narrow domain, broad portfolio.** The domain is restricted to "new materials and manufacturing technologies," justified as being **"upstream of all these problems"** — climate, health, poverty. Reinhardt explicitly distinguishes this specialisation from DARPA's breadth. Within the domain it runs five-plus simultaneous programmes.

So it is neither an FRO (one bottleneck, one organisation) nor a broad institute. Narrowness is enforced by a **domain boundary plus a per-programme budget**, not by a single technical endpoint. Targets work "too long-term, unjustifiable, multidisciplinary or public-goods oriented for other institutions."

**The upstream argument is the interesting move and worth stealing.** Rather than claiming breadth because problems are connected, it picks the layer that is causally upstream of many problems and goes narrow *there*. That is a way of serving many domains without spanning them — and it is a better answer to Q2 than anything the original sketch offers.

## Kill mechanism

Per-programme budget and clock, plus a **declared ~10% success rate**. Declaring the expected failure rate up front is itself a governance device: it makes killing a programme the expected outcome rather than an admission.

## Verdict, and the structural reason

**Too early.** No shipped technical results verified; programmes described as active. The declared 10% success rate also means most programmes are expected to fail, which makes underperformance and design-as-intended hard to distinguish ex post — a real evaluation problem for anyone citing this model.

## Against me

- **It solves the breadth problem by refusing it.** The claim is not "these ten things belong together" but "this one layer is upstream of them." If that move is available for the manifesto's problems, it is a stronger argument than the one currently on the table — and if it is available, the ten departments are the wrong structure.
- **No verified output yet.** Cite the *design*, not the results.

## Implication

**Q2, answer-shape 3, plus a fourth option:** find the upstream layer. The question to ask of the manifesto's ten domains is not "what do they share?" but **"what sits underneath all of them that nobody is funded to build?"** That question has a chance of producing a narrow answer with broad reach — which is exactly the shape that survives contact with the evidence in this folder.
