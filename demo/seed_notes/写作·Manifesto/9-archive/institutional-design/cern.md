# CERN

> **The bottleneck.** No single Western European state could afford a proton accelerator at the energy frontier, and postwar national physics budgets were about to lose their best people to the United States permanently.

The reference case for cross-border binding. Everything in `00-argument/open-questions.md` Q3 is measured against it.

## Facts

**Founding instrument.** Convention signed **Paris, 1 July 1953**; in force **29 September 1954**. The entry-into-force threshold is itself a design decision: seven ratifications totalling **at least 75% of contributions**, and **Switzerland had to be among them** — a structural veto for the seat state. Twelve founding members.

**The chain that produced it** runs through UNESCO and then out of it: de Broglie's proposal at Lausanne (December 1949) → **I. I. Rabi's resolution** at the UNESCO General Conference in Florence, June 1950, authorising UNESCO to "assist and encourage the formation of regional research laboratories" → intergovernmental meeting in Paris, December 1951 → provisional council agreement, February 1952, which coined the acronym → first Council meeting held **at UNESCO**, 5–8 May 1952. Note that a *US* physicist, acting as a US delegate to UNESCO, tabled the enabling resolution for a *European* laboratory. Note also that UNESCO then let go — see `unesco-natural-sciences.md`.

**Funding.** Contributions assessed on "average net national income at factor cost of each Member State for the three latest preceding years" (Art. VII.1.b), reviewed every three years, with adjustments permitted for lower per-capita-income members. **The formula is automatic.** There is no annual political renegotiation of who pays what. 2026 scale: member state contributions **CHF 1,232,149,900** against a total budget of **CHF 1,515M**; Germany 20.44%, UK 15.07%, France 12.96%, Italy 9.64%, Spain 7.01%. Staff 2,704 (2024); ~12,406 users from 80+ countries.

**Governance.** Council is the highest authority — at most two delegates per state, **one vote per state**, simple majority by default, two-thirds for major matters; 25 members and 10 associates. The **Scientific Policy Committee** sets the agenda and its members are "appointed by Council on the basis of scientific eminence **without reference to nationality**." Beam time and experiment approval run through the SPC and the Council, **not through national quotas** — which is what stops the machine from being carved into national shares by another route. Director-General appointed by two-thirds majority, normally five years.

## Scope: how wide, and what holds it there

**Narrow, and enforced by treaty text.** Art. II limits the organisation to "nuclear research of a pure scientific and fundamental character," and **any additional programme requires a two-thirds majority of all member states.** Scope creep is not forbidden; it is *priced*. Over seventy years the drift has been in scale — SC (1957, 600 MeV) → PS (1959, 28 GeV) → SPS (1976, 400–450 GeV) → LEP (1989) → LHC (2008) — and not in subject.

This is the cleanest example in the vault of scope enforcement as a **mechanism** rather than an intention.

## What forces disciplines to converge

A **shared artifact**: one machine that experimentalists, theorists, engineers, and eventually computer scientists all had to touch. The World Wide Web came out of the need to move data around that artifact, not out of a mandate to do computing research.

## Binding mechanism

**An indivisible shared asset, hard-wired by three separate provisions.** All three matter; the first alone is not enough.

1. **The asset is physically unsplittable and immovable.** The Convention names the machines as the programme, and fixes the seat at Geneva (Art. I.2), movable only by a two-thirds Council vote. A departing state cannot take a fraction of a synchrotron home.
2. **Exit is legally slow and reputationally visible.** Art. XII permits withdrawal only **after the Convention has been in force seven years**, by written notice, effective at the end of the *following* financial year. There is no fast exit.
3. **The military exclusion is what made shared ownership possible at all.** Art. II.1: the Organization "shall have **no concern with work for military requirements**" and results "shall be published or otherwise made generally available." By statutorily removing the one thing states will not share, the founders removed the reason to demand national control. **This is the clause IIASA had no equivalent of** — and the 1982 US withdrawal fight was precisely about whether shared data was a security transfer.

In practice: a national high-energy physics community's entire career pipeline runs through beam access. Leaving CERN does not save money. It deletes a discipline.

## Kill mechanism

Not applicable in the usual sense, and worth noting as a limit of the analogy. CERN does not need to kill research lines because the machine *is* the research line; what it kills instead is experiments, through SPC review. An institution whose scope is broad cannot borrow this — it has nothing playing the role the accelerator plays.

## Verdict, and the structural reason

**Worked.** Three design features, jointly sufficient, none of which involve a person:

- an **automatic income-indexed contribution formula**, so funding is not re-fought annually;
- an **indivisible asset in a neutral seat state that holds its own ratification veto**;
- a **supermajority requirement to widen scope.**

A mediocre Director-General cannot dissolve a 27-kilometre tunnel. That is the test of a structural explanation.

## Counterfactual

No European W and Z discovery (1983, Nobel 1984); no Higgs observation in Europe (2012); no World Wide Web as CERN released it — Berners-Lee 1989, code released royalty-free April 1993. European experimental high-energy physics would have been an appendage of Brookhaven and Fermilab.

## Against me

**The honest problem: CERN's mechanism may not be available for the manifesto's problems.**

The binding force is a physical object too expensive for one state and impossible to divide. Medicine, energy, environment and AI mostly do not have such an object — their expensive indivisible things are datasets, models, compute and legal permissions, all of which are *copyable*. A copyable asset does not bind anyone; a member can leave with a full copy. Nobody in this vault has built a legal or informational object as unsplittable as a tunnel.

Two consequences. First, invoking CERN as inspiration without producing the equivalent asset is exactly the move the manifesto has to avoid. Second, and more usefully: **if the CERN trick genuinely cannot be repeated in the software era, saying so clearly — and explaining why — is itself a contribution**, and a more original one than another appeal to international cooperation.

Also note the narrowness. CERN is the most successful cross-border scientific institution ever built and it does *one thing*. It is evidence for Q3 and evidence *against* Q2.

## Implication

**Q3.** The manifesto needs an asset that is (i) unreplicable by one member, (ii) indivisible, (iii) under governance the host cannot seize — plus something playing the role of the military-exclusion clause, i.e. a statutory removal of the thing members would otherwise fight to control.
