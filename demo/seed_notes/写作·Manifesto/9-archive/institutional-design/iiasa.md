# IIASA

> **The bottleneck.** Soviet and American analysts could not sit in one room, run the same models on the same data, and publish jointly on energy, environment and industrial systems without at least one government treating it as intelligence transfer.

This is the first note written against the template, and it was chosen because its cross-border mechanism is the least trivial of any case here. It is also the case that most directly threatens the manifesto: IIASA did nearly everything right in *legal* design and still could not hold its founders.

## Facts

**Founding instrument.** Charter signed at the **Royal Society, London, 4 October 1972**, by **twelve scientific institutions — not governments**. Six years of negotiation (1966–1972), spanning the Vietnam escalation and the 1968 Czechoslovak crisis. Legal status under the current Charter: "a non-governmental, multi-national, autonomous scientific institution," constituted as a not-for-profit entity **under Austrian law**. Laxenburg occupied 1973; Austria waived the first three years of dues.

**Founding actors, and the position each acted from.** The positions matter more than the names.

- **Lyndon Johnson, 1966** — public call for US–Soviet collaboration beyond military and space matters; paralleled by **Kosygin** on the Soviet side.
- **McGeorge Bundy** — *former* National Security Adviser, by then president of the **Ford Foundation**, commissioned Roger Levien at RAND to test feasibility. A private foundation head who had just left the White House: credible to Moscow, deniable to Washington.
- **Philip Handler**, President of the **US National Academy of Sciences**, became chief US negotiator — moving the counterparty from "the US government" to "the US scientific community."
- **Jermen Gvishiani** — Soviet deputy minister for science and technology, Kosygin's son-in-law. He is the one who insisted on a **multilateral** rather than bilateral structure, specifically so the German Democratic Republic could be included.
- **Sir Solly Zuckerman** — UK Chief Scientific Adviser, brokered the Royal Society venue. **Howard Raiffa** (Harvard), first Director 1972–1975.

**Why academies rather than governments — the decisive design move.** In 1972 the blocking problem was **East German recognition**: no Western government could sign a treaty alongside a GDR government. Making the signatories *academies and learned societies* dissolved the recognition question, because the Academy of Sciences of the GDR is not a state. The money was routed through the same fiction — Raiffa's description of the US arrangement was that funds flowed "from the National Science Foundation, which is governmental, to the academy, which is nongovernmental." The current Charter still requires one institution per country representing the scholarly community, with representatives who are **not government officials**. Both Germanies held membership: Max Planck Society (FRG) 1972–1983, Academy of Sciences of the GDR 1972–1990.

**Funding.** Original formula: **thirds** — one-third USSR, one-third USA, one-third the other six original members; later members contributed on par with the original six. Initial US commitment **$2M/year**; the USSR promised a matching contribution **in convertible currency**, which was the Soviet side's costly signal. By **1982** the US contribution was down to ~$1M/year (Washington Post). **2025: budget over €33 million, of which just under 30% came from member organizations** — roughly €10M from members against ~€23M from contracts, grants and donations.

**Governance.** Council of one permanent representative per National Member Organization, one vote each; simple majority for ordinary decisions, two-thirds to amend the Charter. Founding compromise on control: an **American Director paired with a Soviet Council Chair**, majority voting with a **Soviet veto on key decisions**, and in practice everything done by consensus rather than formal vote. **Gvishiani chaired the Council October 1972 – June 1987**, nearly fifteen years, spanning five directors — Soviet chairmanship survived the US withdrawal crisis intact. Membership requires a minimum **five-year commitment**, then resignation on **one year's notice**.

## Scope: how wide, and what holds it there

Broad by charter and drifting by design. The objective is "problems of modern societies arising from scientific and technological development," approached through systems analysis, cybernetics, operations research and management science — deliberately unbounded. **Nothing in the Charter constrains subject matter** except the peaceful-purposes clause. Compare CERN, where any new programme requires a two-thirds Council majority.

This was rational at founding: the *point* was contact, not a specific instrument, so breadth was a feature. It became a liability the moment contact stopped being the point. Drift ran methodological-generic → energy and environment → global change and IPCC-adjacent scenario work.

## What forces disciplines to converge

A **shared toolkit** — systems analysis, and later integrated assessment modelling. Not a shared artifact; the models are portable and the castle is rented. Same category as the Santa Fe Institute, with the same consequence: the toolkit travels everywhere and therefore constrains nothing.

## Binding mechanism

**A détente instrument in a legal-fiction wrapper, with no indivisible asset.** Precisely:

- **The binding force was the political value of the bridge itself, not shared property.** Schloss Laxenburg is a rented Austrian castle. The models are portable. The data was, notoriously, the thing the US objected to sharing. Nothing at Laxenburg was too expensive for either superpower to replicate domestically — RAND and its Soviet counterparts already existed.
- **The clever part is the wrapper, not the bond.** Non-governmental membership solved GDR recognition in 1972 and then, unexpectedly, absorbed a hostile US administration in 1982 by letting the seat migrate between private learned societies. It made the institution *survivable* without making exit *costly*.
- **So the bond was exactly as durable as the bargain underneath it.** When the premise expired — temporarily in 1979–82 (Afghanistan, Poland), permanently in 1991 — members simply stopped paying and the institute had no leverage.

**The 1982 crisis, which is the most instructive episode here.** The Reagan administration moved to end US participation, citing Soviet access to US data banks, an espionage allegation against the institute's Soviet secretary, "one-way benefits," and Poland and Afghanistan. A Reagan official's framing is the whole story in one sentence: the institute was "set up not for good scientific reasons but for political ones," and the administration disagreed with its "East–West bridge-building aspect." **Once détente became the objection rather than the justification, the institution had no independent defence.** Reagan wrote personally to Chancellor Kreisky. And then the workaround: because the member was an *institution*, the US did not have to leave — the seat moved from the National Academy of Sciences to the **American Academy of Arts and Sciences, which held it 1982–2003**. The wrapper built for East Germany saved the US membership a decade later.

**After 1991 the founding coalition largely dissolved.** Of the twelve 1972 countries, current membership retains only the USA, Russia, the UK (rejoined 2015), Japan and Germany. France, Italy, Canada, Poland, Bulgaria are absent; of Czechoslovakia's successors only Slovakia (2020). The membership was replaced by an entirely different constituency — Finland and Sweden 1976, Ukraine 1994, Norway 1996, China 2002, Egypt 2003, India 2007, Korea 2008, Vietnam 2013, Iran 2016, Israel 2017, a **Sub-Saharan Africa Regional Member Organization of 18 nations in 2022**, Greece 2026. The institute converted from an East–West bridge into a global-South-inclusive modelling institute. **It survived by replacing its rationale.**

## Kill mechanism

None identified. No programme-termination rule, no appointment clock analogous to Janelia's five-year review or DARPA's PM term. What disciplines IIASA today is that ~70% of a €33M budget comes from competitive contracts — which means it is disciplined by *funders*, not by members, and the cross-national binding is now close to residual.

## Verdict, and the structural reason

**Mixed — and the reason is the absence of an indivisible asset.**

IIASA got the *legal form* right in ways nobody else has matched: non-governmental signatories, a neutral host, a paired American director and Soviet chair, a five-year minimum with one year's notice. It got *sunk cost* wrong. Because members bought access to a conversation rather than a share in a machine, every member's exit cost was one year's dues and a press release.

Set it beside CERN: same era, same continent, same one-state-one-vote council. A state leaving CERN loses a discipline. **Legal ingenuity buys survival; sunk cost buys adhesion.** They are different properties and IIASA had only the first.

## Counterfactual

Canadian spruce budworm forest management (1970s); water pollution work spanning Japan, the USA and the USSR (1980s); the European acid-rain reduction work from the 1990s; the **MESSAGE** energy-systems model, which by IIASA's account has helped over a hundred countries plan their energy systems. Nordhaus credits IIASA with enabling his early climate-change economics. Absent IIASA, that integrated-assessment tradition would likely have been built inside national labs — and would not have carried joint East–West authorship in the 1970s and 80s, which was the point.

## Against me

Three ways this case cuts against the manifesto.

1. **A cross-border institution can be *designed* brilliantly and still fail to bind.** If the manifesto's cross-border answer is a governance arrangement — a charter, a council formula, a neutral host — IIASA is the counterexample that has already run the experiment.
2. **Breadth plus a bridge-building rationale is a trap.** IIASA's mandate looks a lot like the ten-department sketch: many domains, many nations, serving humanity. It stayed alive but its founding coalition evaporated within twenty years, and today it is a well-run contract-funded modelling institute — a good outcome, and not the one it was founded for. That is the realistic modal outcome for a broad multinational institute, and the manifesto should name it as the thing to beat.
3. **The rationale replacement problem.** IIASA survived by changing what it was for. An institution "for the hardest problems facing humanity" has no such escape hatch, because it already has the broadest possible rationale. There is nothing left to fall back to.

## Implication

Bears hardest on **Q3** (cross-border binding), and secondarily on **Q2** (breadth). The design lesson is sharp enough to state as a rule: *no indivisible asset, no adhesion — however good the charter.* The manifesto must name its equivalent of the 27-kilometre tunnel, and the 1982 episode suggests a second, weaker requirement worth having anyway: build the membership so a hostile government can lose a fight with its own scientific community without the institution dying.

## Unverified / open

- The exact composition of the founding twelve: IIASA's own history page and its *Founding IIASA* PDF disagree, and the Germany member page implies both German academies were founders. Date, venue and the count of twelve are solid.
- Departure dates of the lapsed founders (France, Italy, Canada, Poland, Bulgaria).
- **IIASA's practical posture toward Russian institutions after February 2022.** The Russian Federation remains listed as a member, NMO = Russian Academy of Sciences, "member since 1972," and no suspension statement was found. Whether cooperation was curtailed in practice is unresolved and worth chasing — it is the live test of whether the 1972 wrapper still works.
