# ICIC / IIIC — the League of Nations intellectual cooperation system

> **The bottleneck.** None. This is the diagnostic fact of the case: there was no research task that physically required the institution to exist — no instrument, no dataset, no facility, no fellowship pipeline that a national academy could not have run.

The closest historical analogue to a manifesto that describes a broad, international, humanity-serving body without naming what it uniquely enables. Read it first when the ten-department version starts sounding good.

## Facts

**Instruments.** A 1921 Assembly resolution recommended the Council nominate a committee of "not more than 12 members." **ICIC constituted 1922**, first session 1 August 1922 under **Henri Bergson**. Its status: "an advisory organ of the Assembly and the Council" — **no independent decision-making authority whatsoever**. The **IIIC** was established by League decision in September 1924 on a French offer, inaugurated January 1926 in the Palais Royal, and functioned as the ICIC's "executive organ." A late attempt at a treaty basis — the **International Act concerning Intellectual Co-operation, Paris, 3 December 1938** — came sixteen years in. ICIC active 1922–1939; IIIC closed 1940, briefly reopened 1945–46, closed 1946, functions transferred to UNESCO by an agreement concluded before 31 December 1946.

**People.** Chairs: Bergson (1922–25), **Hendrik Lorentz** (1925–28), **Gilbert Murray** (1928–39). Members included **Marie Curie**, **Albert Einstein**, **J. C. Bose**, **Paul Valéry**, **Thomas Mann** — and **Alfredo Rocco**, Fascist Italy's Justice Minister, 1929–1936. All three IIIC directors were French nationals.

**Funding.** The IIIC was "almost only financed by the French government," which offered to bear all costs on condition the headquarters be in Paris. The ICIC itself had no operating budget — a Council-appointed advisory committee serviced by the League Secretariat. It **met once or twice a year**, twelve to eighteen people.

## Scope: how wide, and what holds it there

**Unbounded from birth and never narrowed.** Bibliography, university relations, museums, letters and arts, textbook revision, intellectual property, cinema. There was no scope-enforcement mechanism at all — **because there was no budget to allocate, there was no allocative discipline.** Scope was set by whatever an unpaid committee of eminent individuals found interesting and whatever France was willing to host.

This is the mechanism worth extracting: *an institution with no budget cannot prioritise, because prioritisation is what a budget forces.* An institution with a broad mandate and no money will produce directories, congresses and declarations, reliably, forever.

## Binding mechanism

**A charter, goodwill, and — worse — a single-donor executive.** No state was bound to anything: advisory to the Council, members sitting as individuals, no convention until 1938, no assessed contribution, no asset. The one real financial commitment came from one state, and it bought that state control: France paid, hosted, and supplied all three directors. The result is the pathology historians call the **Paris/Geneva divide** — the institution's centre of gravity sat in a national capital funded by a national treasury, which is exactly what an international institution is supposed not to be. Participation in its literary and artistic work remained "strongly mediated by a handful of Western European countries."

Note the contrast with ICTP, which is also single-patron and *worked*. The difference: ICTP's patron funds a service delivered to non-payers who have no governance claim; the IIIC's patron funded an executive organ that was supposed to represent everyone. **Single-patron funding is survivable; single-patron control of a body claiming to be multilateral is not.**

## Kill mechanism

None. Nothing was ever terminated; the system ended when the League did.

## Verdict, and the structural reason

**Failed. The institution owned nothing and obliged no one.** Three design features are jointly sufficient to explain the outcome without reference to any person:

1. **Advisory status** — outputs were recommendations to a Council under no duty to act. The 1927 international declaration on history textbook revision "was only regionally effective on account of the lack of mechanisms for implementation."
2. **Single-donor financing of the executive organ** — which converts "international" into "French" and gives every other state a reason to discount the product.
3. **Unbounded scope with no allocative budget** — nothing forces prioritisation, so effort disperses. The Commission "remained controversial on account of its numerous activities and lack of concrete results," and the Institute "lacked funds to do more than facilitating networks between existing institutions."

Two further mechanisms worth naming because they are tempting mistakes:

- **Eminence recruited for visibility rather than capability.** The Secretariat deliberately recruited eminent members to enhance public exposure and legitimacy. That is a legitimacy strategy, not a delivery strategy, and it backfired in public: **Einstein resigned in 1923 protesting the committee's inefficacy**, rejoining in 1924 only to deny German chauvinists the use of his resignation.
- **Coordination without leverage.** The Secretariat's section "primarily compiled directories rather than actively coordinating activities, as existing technical organizations resisted absorption."

## Counterfactual

UNESCO's institutional template, archives (115 linear metres, held by UNESCO), premises legacy and personnel cadre; the International Museums Office (1927) lineage in museum standards; the 1927 textbook-revision declaration as ancestor of postwar history-textbook diplomacy. **The one thing it built that survived is the thing requiring no compliance from anyone: an archive.**

## Against me

This case *is* the objection. Strip the dates and the ICIC's founding description is close to the original manifesto sketch: a broad international body of eminent people, spanning many domains, serving humanity, with a charter and a headquarters and no named unique capability. It ran for twenty-four years, involved Bergson, Curie, Einstein and Mann, and produced a filing system.

Nothing about the people was the problem. That is what makes it useful.

## Implication

**Q1 and Q2.** Concretely, it supplies two disqualifying tests any draft must pass:

- **The bottleneck test.** If you cannot name a task that physically requires this institution to exist, you are writing the ICIC.
- **The prioritisation test.** If the institution has no mechanism that forces it to choose — a budget that binds, an asset that constrains, a vote that costs something — breadth will convert into directories.
