# Focused Research Organizations / Convergent Research

> **The bottleneck.** Assembling a 10–30 person interdisciplinary *engineering* team for 3–7 years to build a research tool, dataset or platform that is too unpublishable to sustain an academic career and too unprofitable to sustain a startup.

The most direct live challenge to the manifesto's breadth. The FRO movement's entire selling point is extreme scope narrowing, and it is the fastest-spreading new research-institution model of the last five years.

## Facts

**Founded 2021** by **Adam Marblestone, Tom Kalil and Anastasia Gamick** as a nonprofit incubator, with intellectual origins in Ed Boyden's MIT lab (~2014–2019). **$50M** in commitments from Schmidt Futures and Ken Griffin, March 2023. (Note: Wikipedia lists Eric Schmidt, Wendy Schmidt and Ken Griffin as "founders" — they are the funders.)

Each FRO is a separate nonprofit with a CEO and CTO, targeting **$20–50M over 3–7 years with 10–30 FTE**. Three named exits: **.org** (endowed foundation), **.gov** (state-supported public good), **.com** (licensed to a for-profit).

## Scope: how wide, and what holds it there

**This is the case's whole content, and the argument runs in three layers.**

**Layer 1 — narrowness as a defence mechanism** (Rodriques & Marblestone, FAS Day One Project, 24 Sept 2020). Focused scope prevents **"mission creep and organizational aging."** An FRO must have a "clear, goal-oriented purpose" with "quantitative metrics and/or concrete design goals," "limited in scope and duration." The underlying diagnosis is about *incentives*, not scope: "the academic reward structure favors individual credit and discourages systematic teamwork" while "commercial incentives encourage teamwork but discourage the production of public goods." **Narrowness is what allows a time-bounded organisation to exist at all** — an organisation with a broad mandate has no natural end date and therefore becomes permanent, with permanent-institution pathologies.

**Layer 2 — narrowness as a selection filter.** The finite-input box — 10–30 FTE, $20–50M, 3–7 years — is fixed *first*, and only problems that fit it qualify. Note the direction of causation. Scope narrowness is not a management style; it is an eligibility criterion.

**Layer 3 — the tech tree.** Bottlenecks sit in the *trunk*, not the branches: "we often notice the tips of these branches that bear fruit… We hypothesize that FROs can help stunted parts of the trunk begin to produce thriving, branching limbs." A trunk bottleneck is by construction a single specific blockage, so the organisation targeting it should be single and specific. They estimate **100 to 200** such gaps exist.

**How it is actually enforced — and note the retraction.** The original device was pre-specified DARPA-style milestones. That **failed**: science is not clock-bound. The replacement is a **"North Star" goal with adaptive intermediate targets remapped quarterly**. So enforcement has migrated from *fixed milestones* to *fixed endpoint + fixed budget + fixed clock, with a floating middle*. **The budget and the clock now do most of the work.**

## Kill mechanism

**The clock.** The organisation is designed to end. This is the cleanest kill mechanism in the vault — and it is entirely untested, see below.

## Verdict, and the structural reason

**Too early, with one large asterisk.**

Real things have shipped — PRISM/PRISMA, Forest 1 into an NHS trial, plexDIA, the Lean toolchain in use at DeepMind, Cultivarium's organism portal, the Pharmome portal. Talent turned out not to be the constraint they feared: the mission alone pulled people out of academia and industry.

**But no FRO has publicly completed its term.** Convergent's own October 2025 retrospective says they are "seeing the first wave of FRO transitions" and **names none**. The oldest FROs (E11 Bio, Cultivarium, 2021–22) are at four to five years, inside the window, and Cultivarium is visibly expanding rather than sunsetting. **The .org/.gov/.com exit thesis is, as of August 2026, entirely untested — and the exit is the part of the model that makes narrowness and time-bounding coherent.** This is the single biggest gap in the evidence base and a genuinely useful thing to point out in print.

**What the designers themselves say they got wrong** (Marblestone, Gamick, Wang & Fridman, *Issues in Science and Technology*, 13 Oct 2025):

| assumption | outcome |
|---|---|
| milestones should be DARPA-rigid | **failed** — replaced with North Star + quarterly remapping. "We've discovered that we couldn't import their model wholesale either." |
| scientists can manage projects | **no** — project management is not taught in graduate school; they now hire executive coaches and operational leads early |
| money can be raised mid-stream | **hard** — shifted to securing full budgets upfront |
| proof of concept is the deliverable | **insufficient** — FROs need active user adoption, requiring an "entrepreneurial bent." **Dissemination is not automatic.** |
| talent would be the constraint | **wrong, pleasantly** |

**Criticisms.** Corin Wagen (2023): the **$20M+ scale floor excludes small high-risk questions**, and concentrating a whole organisation on one pick makes selection unusually high-stakes; his counterproposal is for-profit micro-FROs. And the designers' own caution: "FROs should not **replace** any part of the existing scientific ecosystem."

## Against me

- **This model exists, is spreading, has government money behind it in two countries, and its central claim is that narrowness is the point.** Any argument for one broad roof has to engage it directly.
- **Its policy channel is real and specific.** The FAS follow-up (Alice Wu, 2023) asks Congress to grant USDA and NOAA **Other Transactions Authority** so agencies can fund an FRO without a standard grant instrument — that is what "who should do what" looks like in this genre. The White House AI Action Plan (2025) recommended long-term FRO agreements.
- **Its designers publish their own failures in the venue you are targeting.** The *Issues* piece is the template: a self-critical field report from people running the thing. That is a higher evidentiary bar than a vision statement, and it is now the standard in this space.

## Implication

**Q2, adversarially.** Two productive responses, both better than ignoring it:

1. **Accept the box and multiply it.** Ten independently killable, separately funded, time-bounded programmes with a shared operating layer. Breadth becomes portfolio structure rather than mandate — and the argument shifts to *why the shared layer is worth having*, which is a much more defensible claim.
2. **Attack the filter.** The FRO box selects for problems that *fit* — 10–30 people, five years, one tool. Some real bottlenecks do not fit: they need many domains simultaneously, or they are institutional rather than technical. **"What does the FRO filter systematically miss?" is a strong, original, publishable question**, and answering it is the most direct route from this vault to an *Issues* piece.
