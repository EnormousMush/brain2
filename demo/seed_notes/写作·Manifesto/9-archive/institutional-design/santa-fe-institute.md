# Santa Fe Institute

> **The bottleneck.** A physicist and an economist could spend a month in a room arguing about the same model with no department chair, no course load, no tenure clock, and no funding agency asking which discipline's panel should review it.

**The most important case in this vault for Q2.** SFI is the closest existing thing to a deliberately broad, multi-domain research institute organised around problems rather than disciplines. It has run for forty years. Read this note before defending the ten departments.

## Facts

Founded **1984** as the Rio Grande Institute by **George Cowan, David Pines, Stirling Colgate, Murray Gell-Mann, Nick Metropolis, Herb Anderson, Peter Carruthers and Richard Slansky** — all but Pines and Gell-Mann from **Los Alamos**. Independent 501(c)(3); no departments, no degrees. Stated purpose: "a forum to conduct theoretical research outside the traditional disciplinary boundaries of academic departments and government agency science budgets." **The founders left the definition of "complexity" deliberately unclear** to keep the problem space open.

**Funding.** ~$5M/yr in 1995 (half federal, half private, six full-time researchers). FY2019–20: government grants $1.92M then $1.51M; corporate network (ACtioN) ~$1.1–1.2M; gifts and non-government grants $5.3M then $8.3M — i.e. **roughly 85–90% private**. FY2023 revenue $9.2M against expenses $16.7M; FY2024 revenue $51.8M against expenses $17.0M, net assets $84.4M. Revenue is **extremely lumpy** — campaign- and bequest-driven, not annuity-driven, and endowment was only ~$12–14M in FY19–20 against ~$16M of annual expenses.

**Two facts about scale that should recalibrate everything:** SFI runs on about **$17M a year**, roughly 0.4% of DARPA and a fifth of Janelia. And its insulation from short-term *evaluation* is excellent while its insulation from short-term *fundraising* is weak — a genuine structural difference from Bell Labs, DARPA and Janelia, which each sat on a rent, an appropriation or an endowment.

**Governance.** **No permanent or tenured positions at all.** A small resident faculty, postdocs, a large visitor programme, and roughly **100 external ("fractal") faculty** whose primary appointments are elsewhere, across 80+ institutions and 20 countries. Postdocs get **up to three years, with no PI and no assigned project**, and eligibility is a PhD in *any* scientific discipline — structurally more independent than most tenure-track assistant professors.

## Scope: how wide, and what holds it there

**Maximally broad, and nothing holds it anywhere.** Working groups self-assemble; the institute funds convening rather than projects. There is no allocation authority to say no, and no equivalent of DARPA's Heilmeier Catechism.

## What forces disciplines to converge

**A shared toolkit** — agent-based models, power laws, networks, information theory, evolutionary dynamics — plus the removal of the two institutions that enforce disciplinary loyalty (departments and tenure). Four mechanisms are real and worth naming, because they are the transferable part:

1. **No departments, no tenure.**
2. **The no-PI, no-project postdoc.** The people with the most time are the people with no disciplinary supervisor.
3. **The external/fractal faculty model.** SFI *imports* breadth rather than hiring it — no salaries, no bench space. This is the genuinely clever structural innovation and the one most worth copying.
4. **The SFI Studies in the Sciences of Complexity book series**, which gives a cross-disciplinary workshop a citable output format — which is what disciplinary CVs need.

**And this is exactly where the critique lands.** Bell Labs had breadth disciplined by an artifact; DARPA by a customer; Janelia by a technique. SFI has breadth disciplined by a toolkit and a taste community — and a toolkit portable enough to travel everywhere provides no stopping rule. Crutchfield and Mitchell, both SFI, said so in 1995: **"If a theory applies to everything, it may really apply to nothing."**

## Kill mechanism

**None.** Nothing ends a line of work. Nobody has the authority to say a research direction is finished. SFI's own leadership recognised the problem in 2001 — internal debate recorded over whether the institute should "change its topic of study: complexity may have to go" to avoid institutional hardening — and then did not do it. Janelia faced the same risk and *did* impose scheduled research-area turnover. **That comparison is the single clearest design lesson in this vault.**

## Verdict, and the structural reason

**Mixed, tilting to "real but oversold." SFI reliably produces fields and people; it does not reliably produce settled results.**

**The strongest case that it worked:**

- **Complexity economics is a real, ongoing research programme SFI created.** The 1987 economics programme (funded by Citicorp CEO John Reed) produced physicist–economist collaborations that a Harvard review found "had an impact both on how economics is done and on possible career paths for physicists." W. Brian Arthur's increasing-returns work is the canonical output.
- **It spun out working technology.** Prediction Company (Santa Fe, 1991 — Farmer, Packard, McGill) built statistical-learning trading systems; O'Connor contract 1992 → Swiss Bank → **bought outright by UBS in 2005** → Millennium 2013 → closed 2018. **One losing year in twenty-six.**
- **Urban scaling.** Bettencourt, Lobo, Helbing, Kühnert and West, PNAS 2007, created a quantitative subfield that did not previously exist.
- **It seeded fields that outgrew it** — artificial life, agent-based modelling, network science, econophysics, complexity economics. The pattern is consistent: SFI incubates, universities absorb.
- **All of it on $5–17M a year, for forty years, with no tenure and no endowment to speak of.**

**The strongest case that it produced atmosphere.** Note who is saying it — four of the harshest critics are SFI insiders or founders. This is not an outside hatchet job.

| critic | argument |
|---|---|
| **Jack D. Cowan** (U. Chicago; **SFI founder**) | "There has been tremendous hype." Research tends "to degenerate into computer hacking." The field's real discovery: "it's very hard to do science on complex systems." |
| **Herbert Simon** (Nobel, CMU) | "Most of the people who talk about these great theories have been infected with mathematics… I think you'll see a bust on the notion of unification." |
| **John Maynard Smith** (Sussex) | Artificial life is "basically a fact-free science." |
| **Rolf Landauer** (IBM) | Complexologists chase a "magic criterion" that "doesn't exist." |
| **Sidney Nagel** (U. Chicago) | Per Bak's sandpile model — the flagship self-organized-criticality result — does not describe actual sandpiles. |
| **Crutchfield & Mitchell** (both SFI) | On the edge of chaos: "It's a moving target." And: "If a theory applies to everything, it may really apply to nothing." |
| **Melanie Mitchell** (SFI) | On a unified theory of complex systems: "I don't even know what that would mean." |

**The technical demolitions of its signature empirical claims:**

- **Scale-free networks.** Broido & Clauset, "Scale-free networks are rare" — ~1,000 datasets; only **4% show the strongest evidence** of scale-free structure, **52% the weakest**; social networks essentially never. **Aaron Clauset is SFI external faculty.** The most damaging critique of a complexity-science signature claim came from inside the building.
- **Urban scaling.** Leitão, Miotto, Gerlach & Altmann (*Royal Society Open Science*, 2016) conclude that whether urban scaling is nonlinear "crucially depend[s] on the fluctuations contained in the data, on how they are modeled, and on the fact that the city sizes are heavy-tailed distributed" — i.e. **the headline result is a modelling choice.** Arcaute et al. (2015) show exponents depend on how city boundaries are drawn.
- **The sociological indictment.** Fabrizio Li Vigni, "The failed institutionalization of 'complexity science'," *History of Science* (2021): after forty years and enormous visibility, complexity science never became "a well-established and autonomous research and educational field, capable of reproducing itself through professional institutions." **No departments, no degree pipeline, no self-reproducing profession.**
- **The boom–bust pattern.** John Horgan (1995, and again in 2012): cybernetics → catastrophe theory → chaos → fractals → complexity, each promising unification and retreating. Seth Lloyd documented **more than forty definitions of "complexity."** SFI's own news page reproduced Horgan's six criticisms without rebuttal.

**Synthesis.** Of SFI's four best-known empirical universals — self-organized criticality, edge of chaos, scale-free networks, urban and metabolic scaling — **the first two have been abandoned even by insiders and the last two are actively contested in top journals, partly by SFI-affiliated researchers.** That is a poor hit rate for a forty-year programme premised on the existence of universal laws of complex systems.

The strongest defence is that SFI was never meant to produce settled results — it is a field incubator with a deliberately vague charter and a rolling supply of unsupervised postdocs, and should be judged on whether the fields it spawned are alive elsewhere. On that metric it clearly worked. **But notice that this defence concedes Li Vigni's point rather than refuting it: SFI produced a diaspora, not a discipline.**

## Counterfactual

The 1987 economics programme and the physicist-into-economics career pathway; Prediction Company and one strand of quantitative statistical-learning finance; the SFI Studies series as publication substrate for early artificial life and complexity economics; the post-2007 quantitative urban-scaling literature, including its critics. **Not** in the counterfactual: genetic algorithms (Holland, Michigan, pre-SFI), chaos theory, information theory. Be careful with SFI-adjacency claims.

## Against me

This is the strongest single argument against the manifesto as originally conceived, and it should be met head-on rather than avoided.

SFI is the deliberate, well-funded, forty-year, high-talent experiment in exactly the thing the manifesto proposes: many domains, one roof, problems not disciplines, freedom from disciplinary review. Its own founders and faculty say the results were oversold. Its signature claims are contested. It never reproduced itself as a field.

If the manifesto proposes breadth, it has to explain **what it has that SFI did not**. On the evidence, the two missing pieces are a **convergence device stronger than a toolkit** and a **kill mechanism**. Anything else is a restatement of SFI's charter with more departments.

## Implication

**Q2, decisively.** Two design requirements follow, and they are cheap to state and expensive to satisfy:

1. **A convergence device that is not a shared vocabulary.** A shared artifact (Bell Labs, Janelia) or a shared paying customer (DARPA). A shared formalism is not sufficient — that experiment has been run.
2. **A kill mechanism.** Something ends a research area on a schedule, and someone has the authority and the incentive to use it. Janelia's ~15-year research-area turnover is the working example.

What is worth stealing regardless of what the manifesto becomes: **the external/fractal faculty model** (breadth without paying for it), the **no-PI postdoc**, no tenure, no departments, and private funding to escape disciplinary review panels.
