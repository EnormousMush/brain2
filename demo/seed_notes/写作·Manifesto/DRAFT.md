# The Agreement Problem

*A manifesto for an institution that would belong to everyone*

> "We appeal, as human beings, to human beings: Remember your humanity, and forget the rest."
> — the Russell–Einstein Manifesto, 9 July 1955

---

## I. What we have already agreed

In the thirty months before this essay was written, the proposition that artificial intelligence must benefit all of humanity — not a company, not a country, not a class — was endorsed by nearly everyone on Earth with the power to make it true.

All 193 member states of UNESCO adopted it as a formal recommendation. Fifty-eight governments signed a version of it at Paris in early 2025; some eighty-eight signed another at New Delhi a year later. The chief executives of frontier AI laboratories wrote long essays affirming it. Pope Leo XIV devoted his first encyclical to it — forty-two thousand words on human dignity in the age of intelligent machines. And the President of China announced an intergovernmental organization in its name, headquartered in Shanghai, with training programmes and regional centres attached.

In the same thirty months, no one proposed an instrument that would actually do it.

This is not an accusation of hypocrisy. Most of the people who said these words meant them. The Pope meant them. The laboratory executives, by every appearance, meant them. The diplomats who negotiated the summit declarations meant them at least as much as diplomats mean anything. Sincerity is not what is missing.

What is missing is an addressee. A proposition endorsed by everyone with power imposes an obligation on no one in particular. It cannot be violated, because there is no act that would count as violating it; it cannot be fulfilled, because no one has said what fulfilling it would consist of. It has become something stranger and more comfortable than a lie: a truth that functions as a substitute for the thing it describes.

I want to argue that this is not an accident, and not a delay — that the agreement and the absence are structurally related. The consensus is not a step toward the instrument. It is what makes the instrument's absence invisible. And then I want to propose the instrument.

## II. How a consensus keeps its promise from being kept

We have run this experiment before, at planetary scale, with the results published.

In 2015, every member state of the United Nations adopted the Sustainable Development Goals: seventeen goals, one hundred and sixty-nine targets, the most comprehensive statement of collective human intention ever produced. The goals were non-binding, self-reported, and reviewed voluntarily. In 2022, a team of researchers led by Frank Biermann surveyed more than three thousand scientific studies of the goals' effects and delivered the verdict in one word: the impact had been "largely discursive." The goals changed how institutions talked. There was little evidence they changed what institutions did — legislation, budgets, allocation. By the United Nations' own 2026 accounting, of the targets with sufficient data, barely a third are on track; nearly half are moving too slowly; about one in seven has fallen below its 2015 baseline. [C-supported: SDG Report 2026; Biermann et al., *Nature Sustainability*, 2022]

The standard reading of this record is failure of will. I think the mechanism is more interesting, and worse. A universal aspiration performs the moral work that an instrument would otherwise have to perform. Once the right thing has been said — unanimously, repeatedly, at summit after summit — the question feels attended to. The consciences of the powerful are answered by the statement itself. What remains is implementation, and implementation, unlike aspiration, has costs, addressees, and enemies.

Consider who would have to prefer otherwise. The laboratories gain everything from the aspiration and lose nothing by it: stating it is free, believing it is easy, and it forecloses no product decision. The signatory states commit one signature; the declarations they sign contain, by design, no verification body — the New Delhi Declaration says so on its face. Even the critics need the aspiration, since the charge that AI's benefits are being hoarded presupposes the agreement that they should not be. Every actor in the system is better off with the sentence than without it, and no actor is made responsible by it. A proposition like that does not decay. It is stable — load-bearing, even. It holds up a world in which nothing further needs to happen.

The philosopher Hans Jonas observed in 1979 that our inherited ethics was built for a world where human action was local, short-range, and reversible — and that technology had quietly voided each of those assumptions while the ethics stood unrevised. Something similar has happened here, one level up: our inherited *machinery of agreement* — declarations, summits, principles — was built for a world in which the hard part was getting powerful actors to say yes. AI has inverted the problem. Everyone has said yes. The machinery has nothing left to do, and it keeps doing it.

## III. What the word "benefits" is hiding

The consensus survives scrutiny because of a single word that everyone reads and no one examines. The documents all promise that AI's *benefits* will reach everyone. But "the benefits of AI" is not one kind of thing. It is at least five, and they behave so differently that promising "benefits" without saying which is like promising "transportation" without saying whether you mean a bus ticket or a driving lesson.

**Outputs** are the first kind: a diagnosis, a forecast, a translation, a tutoring session. Outputs are cheap to distribute — an API call away — and every serious document in the consensus is, on inspection, about them.

**Access** is the second: the standing ability to obtain outputs. Access can be granted at scale and revoked at will. It exists at the pleasure of whoever runs the model.

**Capability** is the third, and here the ground shifts: the ability to build, adapt, evaluate, and retrain the systems themselves — the compute, the data, the people, the institutional muscle. Capability is what turns a population from consumers of intelligence into producers of it.

**Agenda-setting** is the fourth: the power to decide which problems the systems are pointed at — whose diseases, whose languages, whose harvests.

**The standing to refuse** is the fifth, and the least discussed: the ability of a community to decline a deployment without simply bearing whatever cost follows.

Read the consensus documents with these five in hand and a pattern appears: the promises concern the first two kinds almost exclusively. [C7 — this claim requires the document audit before publication; it is stated here as a hypothesis the reader can test] The encyclical, the most morally serious text in the corpus, argues magnificently for human dignity and, as the Brookings Institution noted in its review, specifies no affirmative mechanism for distributing gains at all — it prevents harm rather than distributing anything. [C3] The laboratory essays assign hopeful probabilities to global benefit-sharing and describe no instrument. [C4] The summit declarations promise inclusion and establish no obligations. What every one of these documents means by "benefits," when it means anything, is outputs and access.

And the difference matters more than any quantity does. Distributing outputs and distributing capability are not two amounts of the same generosity; they are two different relationships between the giver and the receiver. A population that receives outputs receives answers to questions someone else chose to ask. It is served — and it is dependent, and its dependence deepens precisely as the outputs improve. A population that receives capability changes what questions get asked. The first arrangement can be, and historically has been, entirely compatible with domination. There is a mature philosophical literature adjacent to this point — Amartya Sen built an entire framework on the difference between giving people goods and giving people capabilities [C8 — the relation to Sen must be researched and either built upon or distinguished before publication] — but the AI consensus has not absorbed it. The most consequential technology of the century is being governed by promises that do not distinguish a bus ticket from a driving lesson.

The concentration is not hypothetical. Researchers at the Oxford Internet Institute counted the world's specialised AI data centres in 2025 and found them in thirty-two countries. More than one hundred and fifty countries have none. The United States and China together control over ninety per cent of the specialised compute; by one reported comparison, a single Harvard institute holds more AI compute than every African-owned facility combined. [C6 — reported; the primary source must be read before publication] Whatever one believes about how transformative these systems will become, the *capacity to make them* is presently more concentrated than almost any strategic resource in history — more concentrated than oil, which at least occurs where it occurs. And unlike oil, this concentration is recursive: the systems improve the productivity of the people who build systems. Left alone, the gap does not close. It compounds.

One legitimate response is that AI is, in the useful phrase of Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, "normal technology" — a general-purpose technology like electricity, whose diffusion is governed by boring institutional facts, not a species-level rupture requiring species-level answers. I accept more of this argument than most people writing manifestos do. But notice what it implies rather than what it dismisses: normal technologies have diffusion histories, and those histories are precisely the record of capability arriving generations after outputs, or never. Electricity reached the colonised world as lit streets in the governor's quarter long before it arrived as engineering schools. If AI is normal technology, the capability gap is not an exotic risk; it is the *default outcome*, running on historical rails we have seen before. The normal-technology thesis does not dissolve the distribution problem. It schedules it.

## IV. Which "together"

The word doing the heaviest work in every one of these documents — including, until this point, this one — is *together*. Humanity should advance together. But the word hides at least four different claims, and they cannot all be the priority, because they call for different machinery.

It can mean that progress's *benefits* reach everyone — the saturated sense, the one 193 states have endorsed, which the previous section argued means outputs.

It can mean that progress's *production* includes everyone — that the making of the century's defining technology is not the private activity of two countries and a dozen campuses.

It can mean that progress's *direction* is agreed — that a fragmented species converges on where all this should go.

And it can mean that progress does not proceed *by leaving some of humanity behind* — that the gains are not purchased at the cost of populations who never consented to the purchase.

The third sense is the one the summits pursue, and I think it is the wrong one to put first — not because convergence is undesirable but because it is unavailable. A species divided into rivalrous states does not agree on directions; the past three years of summit history — the safety agenda renamed to an action agenda renamed to an impact agenda, the word "safety" quietly removed from the names of two national institutes within eighteen months of their founding — is a record of what happens when direction is asked to carry the weight. [C-supported]

The first sense, benefits, we have seen: it is what everyone already promises, and promising it costs nothing.

The claim of this essay is that the second sense is the load-bearing one — **together means that the production of progress includes humanity, not merely its consumption** — and that the fourth sense follows from the second and cannot be secured without it. A population with capability cannot be left behind, because it can build for itself what others decline to build for it; its standing to refuse is backed by an ability to proceed alone. A population with outputs alone can be left behind by a pricing decision. If you secure the second sense of together, the first and fourth come with it. No other sense has that property.

This is also where the age's most rigorous pessimism connects. The researchers who formalised the idea of "gradual disempowerment" showed that even perfectly aligned AI can end badly for humanity: as economies and states stop depending on human participation, human influence erodes without anyone ever deciding it should. Their argument is framed as loss — what happens when humanity ceases to function as a collective agent. The constructive counterpart has not been written: what arrangement of the world would keep humanity a *producing* participant in its own progress rather than a client of it? That is the question the rest of this essay tries to answer, and it is not answerable by declaration. It requires a thing that exists.

## V. The proposal

I want to describe an institution. I am aware of the company this places me in — the world does not obviously need another proposal for a global body serving humanity, and the historical record of broad, high-minded international institutions is a warning I take seriously; I will come back to it. The description first, then the two design problems that decide whether it deserves to exist, then what would make all of this wrong.

The institution is a research organisation — that and nothing else. It earns no profit; it pays its people and returns its surplus to research. Its purpose is the problems that are hardest and most common to the species: the diseases medicine has abandoned as intractable; energy without scarcity; the restoration of the planetary environment; the continuity of civilisation beyond Earth; artificial intelligence itself — its safety, its ethics, and its use in service of everything else on this list; the daily unglamorous suffering of ordinary people everywhere; shared prosperity and the collective exit from poverty, including the food, water and education that poverty forecloses; law and universal justice, the repair of the legal gaps through which the powerful step; and the integrity of knowledge itself — because every other pursuit on this list fails if human beings can no longer establish facts in common. Nine branches; a tenth deliberately left open, because a century is long and the list of hardest problems will not hold still. [The branch structure is developed in full elsewhere; what matters here is not the taxonomy but the two design problems below.]

Each branch is led by one person, chosen and replaced by vote within the branch — and structurally unable to profit from the seat: bounded term, no equity, an exit into service rather than wealth. The whole is coordinated by an overall leader who must rotate on the order of a decade or two, so that the institution is always ultimately steered by someone young enough to live with the consequences. An administration under that leader exists for one purpose beyond the ordinary ones: to make the branches' collaboration actual rather than nominal. None of this governance is decorative; each piece answers a failure recorded in the history of institutions like this one. Bounded leaders can afford to end things — the term limit is not about youth but about the freedom to kill a failing programme, a freedom lifetime leaders never have. Election within the branch prevents the capture of the whole by the head. And the requirement that leaders be structurally unable to gain is the honest, checkable version of an old and unverifiable demand — that leaders be willing to die for the people. Structure is what sincerity looks like when it can be audited.

So far, I concede, this could be the mission statement of a dozen institutions that already exist and disappoint. Two design problems separate it from them — or fail to. Everything turns on these two.

**The first problem is money, and the answer is the commons above our heads.**

Every institution of this ambition has been owned by its money. The record is uncomfortably consistent. The research institutes funded by billionaires last exactly as long as the billionaire's attention — one, funded at half a billion dollars, closed in under two years when its backers' mood changed. The international institutes funded by member states last exactly as long as the geopolitical bargain — the great East-West scientific bridge of the Cold War kept its charter and lost its founders within a generation of the bargain's expiry, surviving only by becoming something else. The single-patron model survives longest — one physics centre in Trieste has served the developing world for sixty years on the statutory generosity of Italy — but a single patron is a single point of failure, and the patron's parliament votes every year. [C-supported: the archive, `9-archive/institutional-design/`] Owned money means owed obligations. An institution that intends to serve all of humanity impartially cannot be a client of any part of it. This is not a moral nicety; it is the specific mechanism by which every predecessor died or shrank.

There is exactly one category of wealth on or near Earth that no one owns, and it is most of what exists. The Outer Space Treaty — in force since 1967, ratified by 118 states including every spacefaring power — declares in its first article that the exploration and use of outer space "shall be the province of all mankind," and in its second that no celestial body may be appropriated by any nation, by sovereignty, use, occupation, "or any other means." [C11] The minerals of the asteroids, the energy that falls unclaimed through space — the law of nations has already, remarkably, assigned this to everyone. It is the only property regime on the books under which "for all humanity" is not a donor's promise but an owner's right.

So the proposal, stated plainly: fund the institution from extracted space resources, and treat that funding not as revenue but as *restitution* — the commons, returned to its owners in the form of research on their behalf. An institution financed this way is not grateful to anyone. Its independence is not a governance aspiration written in a charter; it is a property fact. And I should say the quiet implication out loud: the reason no institution like this has ever existed is not that no one thought of it. It is that on Earth, every resource base large enough to sustain one was enclosed centuries ago. The material precondition for a truly unowned institution has only now — with the first commercial asteroid missions, the falling cost of launch — begun to exist. This is why the proposal was impossible in 1955 and is merely absurd today. Absurd is progress.

Honesty requires the complication. The space commons is being enclosed as I write. The Moon Agreement, which would have made the commons explicit as the "common heritage of mankind," was ratified by eighteen states — none of them spacefaring. The United States legislated in 2015 that its companies may own what they extract; Luxembourg followed in 2017; the tension with the treaty's second article is unresolved and, for now, unlitigated. [C12] The enclosure of the last commons is happening the way enclosures always happen: quietly, legally, first-come. I do not regard this as a refutation of the funding model. I regard it as the institution's first case. An organisation with a branch devoted to law and universal justice, funded by the commons, has standing — in the oldest sense of the word — to argue the commons' side. The institution's foundation and its first fight are the same fight. There is a version of this essay in which that is a weakness to be hidden. I think it is the entire point: someone must be constituted to argue for the species' property before the property is gone, and at present, no one is.

**The second problem is what holds it together, and the answer is a rule about sharing.**

The other way institutions of this kind die is drift: breadth without a binding force. Here too the record is consistent, and I will not pretend it away — it is the strongest objection to everything in this section. The League of Nations built a committee of the most luminous minds alive — Einstein, Curie, Bergson — to organise intellectual cooperation for humanity, and it produced, in twenty-four years, mostly directories and declarations, because no task physically required it to exist. A famous institute in Santa Fe assembled brilliant people across every discipline around the idea of complexity, and forty years later its own faculty concede the results were mostly a diaspora of ideas rather than a body of settled findings — breadth disciplined by nothing but a shared vocabulary. The pattern generalises: institutions whose parts share only a mission produce atmosphere. Institutions whose parts share an *asset* — a machine no member could build alone, as at CERN; an instrument, a dataset — produce results, because the asset disciplines everyone who touches it and makes leaving expensive. A state that exits CERN does not save money; it deletes its own high-energy physics. [C-supported: the archive]

The institution's asset is a network — and I mean this as infrastructure, not metaphor. Its foundation is the universities: the laboratories, professors, students and societies of every country, connected to companies, to institutions, and to individuals, under one constitutional rule — **what enters the network is shared without reserve.** Not shared after patenting, not shared after publication, not shared with allies: shared, at once, with the network entire. This rule has an existence proof at planetary scale. In 1996 the leaders of the Human Genome Project agreed at Bermuda that every DNA sequence assembly longer than a thousand bases would be deposited in public databases within twenty-four hours of its generation — before publication, before patent, unconditionally, the whole genome into the public domain. It contravened every incentive orthodox science recognises, and it held, and it became the constitution of an entire field. [C13] A sharing rule, voluntarily adopted and collectively enforced, can be an institution's load-bearing wall.

The network carries two different things, and the distinction of section III is built into its architecture. Through one pipe flow *outputs* — when a branch achieves a result, a therapy, a method, it moves to the point of use at the speed of the network: to the hospitals, to the grids, to the classrooms. Through the other pipe flows *capability* — the network is made of universities precisely because universities are where producers are grown; every node is a place where people learn to build, not merely to receive. An institution that took seriously the argument of this essay could not be structured any other way: it must distribute the driving lessons, not only the bus tickets, and its physical shape — a federation of the world's teaching institutions rather than a headquarters with field offices — is that commitment made concrete.

Is the network a true asset, in the CERN sense — is it expensive to leave? I do not yet know, and I would rather admit the open question than decorate it. Networks can be forked; a defecting faction leaves with a copy of yesterday. What it cannot take is tomorrow: under the Bermuda rule the asset is not the archive but the *live feed* — the stream of unreserved sharing from ten branches at once — and a fork receives the past while forfeiting the future. Whether that forfeiture is costly enough to bind is, I think, the single most important unsolved design problem in this proposal, and I state it as such. [S7]

**What the institution asks of its people** is the one thing I will carry over from the language of belief: that humanity and civilisation stand above every other loyalty. But this is a selection effect to be engineered, not an oath to be sworn. The institutions that tried creeds got performances of creed. The institutions that stated missions sharp enough — the genome project, the focused research organisations of the 2020s — discovered that the mission itself did the selecting; the people who came, came for it. State the mission at full sharpness and the belief test becomes redundant: *we serve all humans, for the greater good of humans, and the work is the test.*

## VI. What would make this wrong

A proposal that cannot fail is not a proposal, so let me say exactly where this one is falsifiable.

The diagnosis is wrong if capability is diffusing on its own. If, five years from now, frontier-scale systems are being trained in fifty countries rather than a handful — if the thirty-two-country map has become a hundred-country map without any instrument like this one — then the consensus was sufficient after all, the normal-technology optimists were right about diffusion's speed and not only its logic, and this essay overestimated the gravity of the moment. I do not expect this, but it is checkable, and I commit to it as the test.

The distinction is wrong if outputs turn out to be enough — if populations with access but no capability show, on the outcomes that matter, no dependence, no agenda exclusion, no vulnerability to revocation. That too is checkable, in the health systems and school systems of the next decade.

And the proposal is wrong if the two design answers fail on their own terms: if space resources cannot be extracted at institution-sustaining scale within a generation [the economics are undemonstrated and this essay does not pretend otherwise — CHECK], or if the network rule proves forkable — if the live feed does not bind. These are engineering and institutional questions, not matters of faith, and they will resolve within the lifetime of everyone reading this.

What I am not willing to grant is the objection from company: that because a hundred declarations have already said "for all humanity," a hundred-and-first document must be empty too. This document differs from those in one auditable way. Every prior text in the consensus asks the reader to *agree*. This one asks the reader to *build* — and specifies the property regime, the constitutional rule, and the failure conditions of the thing to be built. Agreement was never the scarce resource. It is, as section II argued, the surplus commodity of our age. The scarce resource is an addressee, and an institution is what an addressee looks like.

## VII. As human beings

Seventy-one years ago, eleven scientists — nine of them Nobel laureates, one of them signing seven days before his death — published a short document about a technology their own hands had built. They held no office. They represented no one. They said so, in the second paragraph, and the saying of it was the source of their authority: "We are speaking on this occasion, not as members of this or that nation, continent or creed, but as human beings, members of the species Man, whose continued existence is in doubt." They asked for almost nothing — a conference — and the ask was so small that a single private citizen funded it four days later, and the institution it seeded outlived them all and shared a Nobel Peace Prize.

I hold no office either. I represent no laboratory, no state, and no fund; nothing in this essay is anyone's product. I am a student who has worked inside the research systems of the two countries that between them hold ninety per cent of the machinery of the new intelligence, and who has watched, at the working level, how easily "together" breaks — at a data-use agreement, at a border, at a language. [GAP — one first-hand incident, in one paragraph, goes here: the single detail in this essay no one else can write]

The eleven asked their readers to remember their humanity and forget the rest. In their age the technology was a weapon, and remembering meant refusing a war. In ours the technology is a capability, and remembering means something quieter and harder: refusing to let the making of the future become the private craft of a fraction of the species — insisting that humanity's oldest and only real method, which is to learn together, survives its most powerful invention.

The instrument for that insistence does not exist. It can. Everything above is one way to build it, offered not as a finished design but as the opening position of an argument the species should be having and is not.

We have agreed enough. What we owe each other now is not another agreement. It is an address — a place, funded by what no one owns, bound by a rule of giving everything away, where the answer to "who is responsible for all of humanity's hardest problems" stops being *everyone*, which has always meant no one, and starts being: *here*.
